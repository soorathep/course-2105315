Warm-up · Your missing readings

Pairs · 8 minutes · your homework example

Tell your neighbor the reading you brought: what went missing, and why.

1 · What stopped it A fault, a schedule, a person, or the process itself?

2 · When At random times, at the same time each day, or only in certain conditions?

3 · The value Would the missing value have been higher, lower, or the same as the rest?

Keep question 3 in mind. It decides whether any fill method can be trusted.

Studying alone: write down one example and answer the three questions.

Three reasons a value goes missing

Unrelated to anything A random loss. A data packet is dropped, a cable is knocked. Effect: fewer data, no bias. Statisticians call this MCAR.

Related to something recorded A schedule or a condition you can see. For example, an analyzer that calibrates every night at 04:00. Effect: bias, unless you compare like with like. MAR: the reason is in the data.

Related to the missing value The value itself. The analyzer goes over range when the concentration is high. Effect: bias that no fill method removes. MNAR: the reason is the value that is gone.

The third kind is common in plants: instruments fail, and operators skip samples, exactly when the process is unusual.

What the data can tell you

By time Share missing by hour of day and day of week. A peak at one hour points to a schedule.

By length Single hours or long blocks? Long blocks are outages, and they hold most of the missing hours.

Against another tag Is the gap more likely when a related tag is high? A temperature, a load, a second sensor.

At the edges Are the values just before and after the gap unusually high or low?

These checks can point to the third kind of gap. They cannot prove it: the value that caused the gap is the value you do not have.

Activity 1 · Six gaps, three reasons

Pairs · 12 minutes · no computer needed

A flow signal drops out for a minute at random times when a forklift passes the cable tray.

Unrelated. Short and random: fill short gaps; little bias.

A gas analyzer runs an automatic zero check every night at 04:00 and records nothing.

Recorded: time. Dropping it raises the daily mean. Fill from neighboring hours.

A level gauge stores nothing while the tank is above 95 %.

The value. Any fill stays below 95 %. Report the time above range; use the high-level alarm.

No lab samples are taken on public holidays.

Recorded: calendar. Fine if you compare like days; holiday operation may differ.

Operators skip the shift sample when the unit is upset and they are busy.

The value. The record looks calmer than the plant was.

A pH probe is out for cleaning for three days.

Unrelated, but long. Do not draw a line across three days. Leave it missing or use similar days, and report it.

For each gap: which of the three reasons is it, and what would a plain mean of the remaining data get wrong? Decide before you reveal.

Studying alone: write a reason for each card, then reveal.

Six gaps, the answers

Gap Reason What goes wrong if you ignore it
Forklift dropouts Unrelated Little: fewer data
Zero check at 04:00 Recorded: time of day A daily mean without the night hours is too high
Gauge over range The missing value Every fill stays below the range; the overflow time is lost
No samples on holidays Recorded: calendar Holiday operation is never seen
Samples skipped when upset The missing value The process looks more stable than it was
Probe out for three days Unrelated, but long A straight line across three days invents a calm process

Two of the six cannot be fixed by any method applied to the data alone. They can only be reported, or measured another way.

Count the gaps, or count the hours?

Most gaps in the real CO record are single hours: 145 of 187. They hold 9 % of the missing hours. The 12 gaps longer than two days hold 68 %. A plan for single hours does not cover the outages.
